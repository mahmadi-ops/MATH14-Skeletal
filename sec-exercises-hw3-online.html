    1. An Inverse-Square Field.

    Sketch the vector field \(\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(x,y) = -\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert^3}\) in the plane, where \(\mathbf{r} = \left\langle x, y \right\rangle\text{.}\) Select all that apply.
    🔗
    • All the vectors point toward the origin.
    • Correct.
    • The vectors decrease in length as you move away from the origin.
    • Correct.
    • All the vectors point away from the origin.
    • Incorrect.
    • The vectors increase in length as you move away from the origin.
    • Incorrect.
    • The length of each vector is \(1\text{.}\)
    • Incorrect.

    Solution.

    Since \(\mathbf{F} = -\frac{1}{\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert^3}\,\mathbf{r}\) is a negative multiple of the position vector \(\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) the arrow at each point \((x,y) \ne (0,0)\) points from that point toward the origin. Its length is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lVert \mathbf{F} \rVert = \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert^3} = \frac{1}{\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert^2} , \end{equation*}
    which decreases as you move away from the origin (and is not \(1\)). So the true statements are: all the vectors point toward the origin, and they decrease in length as you move away from it. This is an inverse-square field, like gravity; it is not defined at the origin.
    🔗

    2. A Segment and a Half Circle.

    Curves \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are parametrized as follows: \(C_1\) is \((x(t),y(t)) = (t,0)\) for \(-1 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) and \(C_2\) is \((x(t),y(t)) = (\cos t, \sin t)\) for \(0 \le t \le \pi\text{.}\) Sketch, on a separate sheet of paper, the curves \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) with arrows showing their orientation.
    🔗
    Next, suppose that \(\mathbf{F} = 3x\,\mathbf{i} + (4x + 2y)\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Calculate \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) where \(C\) is the curve \(C = C_1 \cup C_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} =\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    The figure below shows the curves: \(C_1\) runs along the \(x\)-axis from \((-1,0)\) to \((1,0)\text{,}\) and \(C_2\) runs along the upper half of the unit circle from \((1,0)\) back to \((-1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On \(C_1\) we have \(y = 0\) and \(d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{i}\,dx\text{,}\) so only the \(\mathbf{i}\)-component of \(\mathbf{F}\) contributes:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_{-1}^{1} 3x\,dx = \frac{3x^2}{2}\Big|_{-1}^{1} = 0 . \end{equation*}
    On \(C_2\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = -\sin t\,\mathbf{i} + \cos t\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi} \big(3\cos t\,\mathbf{i} + (4\cos t + 2\sin t)\,\mathbf{j}\big)\\ \amp \quad \cdot \left(-\sin t\,\mathbf{i} + \cos t\,\mathbf{j}\right) dt\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi} \left(-\cos t\sin t + 4\cos^2 t\right) dt\\ \amp = 2\pi . \end{align*}
    Hence \(\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 + 2\pi = 2\pi \approx 6.283\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. A Constant Field Along a Segment.

    Sketch the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = 3\,\mathbf{i} + 4\,\mathbf{j}\) and calculate the line integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the line segment from \((-1,3)\) to \((4,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The line integral equals .
    🔗

    Solution.

    The field is constant: every arrow is the same vector \(\left\langle 3, 4 \right\rangle\text{,}\) of length \(5\text{,}\) pointing up and to the right. Parametrize the segment by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle -1 + 5t,\ 3 - 2t \right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle 5, -2 \right\rangle\) and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \int_0^1 (3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot (-2))\,dt\\ \amp = 7 . \end{align*}
    For a constant field this is just \(\mathbf{F}\) dotted with the displacement \(\left\langle 5, -2 \right\rangle\) from the start of the segment to its end.
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Twice Around a Helix.

    Find \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = 3y\,\mathbf{i} - 2x\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) if \(C\) is the helix \(x = \cos t\text{,}\) \(y = \sin t\text{,}\) \(z = t\text{,}\) for \(0 \le t \le 4\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} =\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    Since \(C\) is given by \(\mathbf{r} = \cos t\,\mathbf{i} + \sin t\,\mathbf{j} + t\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) we have \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = -\sin t\,\mathbf{i} + \cos t\,\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Thus
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{4\pi} \left(3\sin t\,\mathbf{i} - 2\cos t\,\mathbf{j} + t\,\mathbf{k}\right)\\ \amp \qquad\qquad \cdot \left(-\sin t\,\mathbf{i} + \cos t\,\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}\right) dt\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{4\pi} \left(-3\sin^2 t - 2\cos^2 t + t\right) dt\\ \amp \quad = -6\pi - 4\pi + 8\pi^2 = 8\pi^2 - 10\pi\\ \amp \quad \approx 47.541 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    6. Two Particles, Two Paths.

    With \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle y, x \right\rangle\text{,}\) find the work done from \((-1,1)\) to \((1,1)\) along the parabola \(C_1: y = 2x^2-1\) and along \(C_2\text{,}\) the bottom half of \(x^2 + (y-1)^2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Work along \(C_1\text{:}\) foot-pounds.
    🔗
    Work along \(C_2\text{:}\) foot-pounds.
    🔗

    Solution.

    On \(C_1\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t, 2t^2-1 \right\rangle\) with \(-1 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 6t^2 - 1\) and \(W = \int_{-1}^{1}(6t^2-1)\,dt = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On \(C_2\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle \cos t, 1+\sin t \right\rangle\) with \(\pi \le t \le 2\pi\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \cos(2t) - \sin t\) and \(W = 2\) again.
    🔗
    The two answers agree because \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla(xy)\) is conservative—revisit this problem after Path Independence, Conservative Fields, and Potential Functions.

    7. A Line Integral Along a Helix.

    For the helix \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle \cos t, \sin t, t/2\pi \right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{,}\) compute \(\displaystyle\int_C x\,dy - y\,dx + dz\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The integrand \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}\) is the constant , and the integral equals .
    🔗

    Solution.

    The integral is \(\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) with \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle -y, x, 1 \right\rangle\text{.}\) Along the helix \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle -\sin t, \cos t, 1 \right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \left\langle -\sin t, \cos t, \frac{1}{2\pi} \right\rangle\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 1 + \frac{1}{2\pi}\) and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_C x\,dy - y\,dx + dz\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2\pi}\right) dt\\ \amp \quad = 2\pi + 1 \approx 7.283 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    For the field of Figure 3.3.3: over which path does \(\mathbf{F}\) perform less work? Type ADC or ABC.
    🔗
    Answer:
    🔗
    Is \(\mathbf{F}\) conservative? Type yes or no.
    🔗
    Answer:
    🔗

    Solution.

    Both paths run along two sides of the square. The horizontal sides \(AD\) and \(BC\) contribute the same, but the vertical side \(DC\) sits where the field is longer than it is along \(AB\text{,}\) and the angle between \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{T}}\) is the same on both. Hence \(\int_{ADC} \gt \int_{ABC}\text{,}\) so \(ABC\) is the path of less work.
    🔗
    Because the two paths share their endpoints and give different work, \(\mathbf{F}\) is not conservative: a conservative field is path-independent.
