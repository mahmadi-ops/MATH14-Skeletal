    1. (3 points).

    Rewrite (do not evaluate) the following integral in cylindrical coordinates:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{2x-x^2}}^{\sqrt{2x-x^2}}\!\! \int_0^{4-x^2-y^2} z\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dz\,dy\,dx . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The limits on \(x\) and \(y\) describe the projection of the solid onto the \(xy\)-plane: \(0 \le x \le 2\) and \(y^2 \le 2x - x^2\text{,}\) that is, \(x^2 - 2x + y^2 \le 0\text{,}\) the disk \((x-1)^2 + y^2 \le 1\) of radius \(1\) centered at \((1,0)\) (Figure 3.12.1). Its boundary circle is \(x^2 + y^2 = 2x\text{,}\) in polar coordinates \(r^2 = 2r\cos\theta\text{,}\) so \(r = 2\cos\theta\text{;}\) the disk is swept out by \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\text{,}\) \(0 \le r \le 2\cos\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The limits on \(z\) run from the plane \(z = 0\) up to the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2 = 4 - r^2\) (which is \(\ge 0\) on the disk, since \(r \le 2\) there). The integrand is \(z\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = zr\text{,}\) and \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{gather*} \int_0^2\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{2x-x^2}}^{\sqrt{2x-x^2}}\!\! \int_0^{4-x^2-y^2} z\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dz\,dy\,dx\\ \quad = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{2\cos\theta}\!\!\int_0^{4-r^2} z\,r^2\,dz\,dr\,d\theta . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the projection \(r = 2\cos\theta\) with \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\text{;}\) 1 point for the \(z\)-limits \(0 \le z \le 4 - r^2\text{;}\) 1 point for the integrand with the factor \(r\) from \(dV\) (\(zr \cdot r = zr^2\)).

    2. (4 points).

    Consider the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{y}\!\!\int_0^{1-y^2} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dx\,dy . \end{equation*}
    Rewrite (do not evaluate) this integral in the following forms.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dz\,dx\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The given limits describe the solid
    \begin{equation*} R:\quad 0 \le y \le 1,\qquad 0 \le x \le y,\qquad 0 \le z \le 1 - y^2 , \end{equation*}
    shown in Figure 3.12.2: it stands on the triangle \(0 \le x \le y \le 1\) of the \(xy\)-plane, under the parabolic cylinder \(z = 1 - y^2\) (which meets the plane \(z = 0\) along the line \(y = 1\)); its other two faces lie in the planes \(x = 0\) and \(x = y\text{.}\) Its projections onto the \(xz\)- and \(yz\)-planes are shown in Figure 3.12.3 and Figure 3.12.4.
    🔗
    For the order \(dy\,dz\,dx\) the outer variable is \(x\text{,}\) which runs over \(0 \le x \le 1\) in all. For a fixed \(x\text{,}\) the conditions \(x \le y\) and \(z \le 1 - y^2\text{,}\) i.e. \(y \le \sqrt{1-z}\text{,}\) can both hold only if \(x \le \sqrt{1-z}\text{,}\) that is, \(z \le 1 - x^2\text{.}\) So the projection of \(R\) onto the \(xz\)-plane is the region under the parabola \(z = 1 - x^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le 1\) (Figure 3.12.3), and for each \((x,z)\) in it, \(y\) runs from \(x\) up to \(\sqrt{1-z}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_R f\,dy\,dz\,dx = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x^2}\!\!\int_x^{\sqrt{1-z}} f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dz\,dx . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the correct outer and middle limits (the projection), 1 point for the inner limits.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(R\) as in the solution of part (a), the outer variable is now \(z\text{,}\) with \(0 \le z \le 1\) in all; for a fixed \(z\text{,}\) \(y\) runs from \(0\) to \(\sqrt{1-z}\) (the projection of \(R\) onto the \(yz\)-plane is the region under \(z = 1 - y^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le y \le 1\text{,}\) Figure 3.12.4), and then the inner variable \(x\) runs from \(0\) to \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_R f\,dx\,dy\,dz = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt{1-z}}\!\!\int_0^{y} f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Check (not required): with \(f = 1\) all three orders give the volume \(\int_0^1 y\left(1 - y^2\right) dy = \frac14\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the correct outer and middle limits (the projection), 1 point for the inner limits.

    3. (3 points).

    Set up (do not evaluate) a double integral for the purpose of computing the area inside \(x^2 + y^2 = 3y\) and outside \(x^2 + y^2 = y + \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both curves are simplest in polar coordinates. Since \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\text{,}\) the first is \(r^2 = 3r\sin\theta\text{,}\) that is, \(r = 3\sin\theta\text{:}\) the circle of radius \(\frac32\) centered at \(\left(0, \frac32\right)\text{,}\) traced once as \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi\text{.}\) The second is \(r^2 = r\sin\theta + r\text{,}\) that is, \(r = 1 + \sin\theta\text{,}\) a cardioid with its cusp at the origin, pointing down; its top point is \(r = 2\) at \(\theta = \pi/2\text{,}\) and it passes through \((\pm 1, 0)\) at \(\theta = 0, \pi\text{.}\) Both curves are symmetric about the \(y\)-axis (Figure 3.12.5).
    🔗
    The curves meet where \(3\sin\theta = 1 + \sin\theta\text{,}\) i.e. \(\sin\theta = \frac12\text{,}\) so at \(\theta = \pi/6\) and \(\theta = 5\pi/6\) (both with \(r = \frac32\text{:}\) the points \(\left(\pm\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}, \frac34\right)\)). For \(\pi/6 \le \theta \le 5\pi/6\) we have \(\sin\theta \ge \frac12\text{,}\) so \(3\sin\theta \ge 1 + \sin\theta\text{:}\) along a ray at angle \(\theta\) the region runs from the cardioid out to the circle. With \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\text{,}\) and using the symmetry about the \(y\)-axis,
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \int_{\pi/6}^{5\pi/6}\!\!\int_{1+\sin\theta}^{3\sin\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp = 2\int_{\pi/6}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_{1+\sin\theta}^{3\sin\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Value (not required): \(A = \pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for both polar equations; 1 point for the intersection angles \(\pi/6\text{,}\) \(5\pi/6\) and the sketch; 1 point for the integral with \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) and the correct inner order (cardioid to circle).

    4. (3 points).

    Rewrite (do not evaluate) the following integral in spherical coordinates:
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-3}^{0}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{9-x^2}}^{0}\!\! \int_{\sqrt{(x^2+y^2)/3}}^{\sqrt{12-x^2-y^2}} yz\,dz\,dy\,dx . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The projection. The limits \(-3 \le x \le 0\text{,}\) \(-\sqrt{9 - x^2} \le y \le 0\) describe the quarter of the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 9\) with \(x \le 0\) and \(y \le 0\text{,}\) the third quadrant (Figure 3.12.6): the rays through it have \(\pi \le \theta \le 3\pi/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The solid. The lower limit on \(z\text{,}\) \(z = \sqrt{(x^2 + y^2)/3}\text{,}\) is the upper nappe of the cone \(3z^2 = x^2 + y^2\text{:}\) in spherical coordinates \(\sqrt3\,\rho\cos\phi = \rho\sin\phi\text{,}\) so \(\tan\phi = \sqrt3\) and \(\phi = \pi/3\text{.}\) The upper limit, \(z = \sqrt{12 - x^2 - y^2}\text{,}\) is the upper half of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 12\text{,}\) which is \(\rho = 2\sqrt3\text{.}\) The solid lies above the cone and below the sphere, so it is the part of the ball \(\rho \le 2\sqrt3\) with \(0 \le \phi \le \pi/3\text{,}\) an ice-cream cone, cut to the quarter \(\pi \le \theta \le 3\pi/2\) (Figure 3.12.7). As a check, the cone and the sphere meet where \(x^2 + y^2 + \frac13\left(x^2 + y^2\right) = 12\text{,}\) that is, on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) (at height \(z = \sqrt3\)), exactly the boundary of the projection.
    🔗
    The integral. With \(y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\) and \(z = \rho\cos\phi\text{,}\) the integrand is \(yz = \rho^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\sin\theta\text{,}\) and \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{gather*} \int_{-3}^{0}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{9-x^2}}^{0}\!\! \int_{\sqrt{(x^2+y^2)/3}}^{\sqrt{12-x^2-y^2}} yz\,dz\,dy\,dx\\ \quad = \int_{\pi}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/3}\!\!\int_0^{2\sqrt3} \rho^4\sin^2\phi\cos\phi\,\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the \(\theta\)-limits; 1 point for \(\phi = \pi/3\) and \(\rho = 2\sqrt3\) (with the solid correctly identified as the part above the cone); 1 point for the integrand including \(\rho^2\sin\phi\text{.}\)

    5. (4 points).

    Without calculating the integrals, decide whether each of the following integrals is positive, negative, or zero. Let \(S\) be the solid bounded by \(z = y^2 - 1\text{,}\) \(z = 1 - y^2\text{,}\) \(x = 0\text{,}\) and \(x = 2\text{.}\) Remember to justify your responses.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S e^x\sin y\,dV\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First locate \(S\text{.}\) The two parabolic cylinders meet where \(y^2 - 1 = 1 - y^2\text{,}\) that is, \(y^2 = 1\text{,}\) so at \(y = \pm 1\text{;}\) between them \(1 - y^2\) lies above \(y^2 - 1\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} S:\quad 0 \le x \le 2,\qquad -1 \le y \le 1,\qquad y^2 - 1 \le z \le 1 - y^2 , \end{equation*}
    a solid with the lens-shaped cross-section of Figure 3.12.8 in every plane \(x = c\text{,}\) \(0 \le c \le 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Zero. \(S\) is symmetric about the plane \(y = 0\text{:}\) replacing \(y\) by \(-y\) changes none of its bounding surfaces, so the reflection \(y \mapsto -y\) maps \(S\) onto itself. The integrand is odd under that reflection, \(e^x\sin(-y) = -e^x\sin y\text{:}\) it is \(\ge 0\) on the half \(y \ge 0\) of \(S\) and \(\le 0\) on the half \(y \le 0\text{,}\) with the values on the two halves mirror images of each other (the factor \(e^x\) is the same at mirror points). The two halves contribute opposite amounts and the integral is \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the correct sign, 1 point for the justification; a correct sign with no or wrong reasoning earns at most \(\frac12\) point. The description of \(S\) by inequalities is part of the justification of both parts.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S \left(z + y^2 - 1\right) dV\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Negative. On \(S\text{,}\) described in the solution of part (a), we have \(z \le 1 - y^2\text{,}\) that is, \(z + y^2 - 1 \le 0\text{,}\) with equality only on the top surface \(z = 1 - y^2\text{,}\) which has zero volume. An integrand that is negative throughout the solid except on a surface of zero volume has a negative integral. (Its value is \(-\frac{64}{15}\text{,}\) but no calculation is expected.)
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for the correct sign, 1 point for the justification; a correct sign with no or wrong reasoning earns at most \(\frac12\) point. The description of \(S\) by inequalities is part of the justification of both parts.

    6. (3 points).

    True or False? If the statement is true, prove it; if it is false, give a counterexample.
    🔗
    Let \(E\) be the solid unit ball \(E = \{(x,y,z) : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1\}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E (x + y + z)^2\,dV = \iiint_E \left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right) dV . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    True. Expand the square:
    \begin{equation*} (x + y + z)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 2xy + 2yz + 2xz . \end{equation*}
    So it suffices to show that
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E xy\,dV = \iiint_E yz\,dV = \iiint_E xz\,dV = 0 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Use symmetry. The unit ball is symmetric about the plane \(x = 0\text{:}\) the reflection \((x,y,z) \mapsto (-x,y,z)\) maps \(E\) onto itself and does not change volumes. The integrand \(xy\) is odd under it, \((-x)y = -xy\text{:}\) the half \(x \ge 0\) of \(E\) and the half \(x \le 0\) contribute opposite amounts, so \(\iiint_E xy\,dV = 0\text{.}\) The same reflection kills \(xz\text{,}\) and the reflection \(y \mapsto -y\) kills \(yz\text{.}\) (Equivalently, in spherical coordinates each cross term carries a factor \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta\text{,}\) \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta\) or \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta\text{,}\) all equal to \(0\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Conclude. Integrating the expansion over \(E\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \iiint_E (x + y + z)^2\,dV\\ \amp \quad = \iiint_E \left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right) dV + 2 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0\\ \amp \quad = \iiint_E \left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right) dV . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Numerical value (not required). In spherical coordinates,
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E \rho^2 \cdot \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = \frac15 \cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = \frac{4\pi}{5} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Grading. 1 point for “True” with the expansion; 2 points for a correct symmetry (or direct spherical-coordinates) argument that the three cross terms integrate to \(0\text{.}\)
