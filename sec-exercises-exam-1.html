    The first exam of Fall 2026, with brief solutions. It had six problems worth 20 points in all, and 65 minutes to do them in. It was a closed-book exam: no aids of one’s own (formula sheets, notes, phones, tablets, smart watches or similar) and no calculators of any kind were permitted. Students were asked to write their work in a neat and organized format, to fully justify every step, and to give exact answers. Try the exam under the same conditions before you look at the solutions. The topics it covers, with links to the notes:

    1. (3 points).

    Two particles move in the plane. At time \(t\) their position vectors are
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{r}_1(t) \amp = \left\langle t,\ t^2 \right\rangle\\ \mathbf{r}_2(t) \amp = \left\langle 2t - 3,\ 2t + 3 \right\rangle, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Find all intersection points of the two paths. (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Solve \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{r}_2(s)\) (different parameters): \(t = 2s - 3\) and \(t^2 = 2s + 3\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} (2s - 3)^2 \amp = 2s + 3\\ \Rightarrow\quad 4s^2 - 14s + 6 \amp = 0\\ \Rightarrow\quad (2s - 1)(s - 3) \amp = 0 : \end{align*}
    \(s = \frac12\) (\(t = -2\)) or \(s = 3\) (\(t = 3\)). Intersection points: \((-2, 4)\) and \((3, 9)\text{.}\) (Equivalently: the parabola \(y = x^2\) meets the line \(y = x + 6\) where \(x^2 = x + 6\text{,}\) i.e. \(x = -2\) or \(3\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find all collision points of the two particles. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Same place at the same time: \(\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \mathbf{r}_2(t)\) gives \(t = 2t - 3\text{,}\) so \(t = 3\text{,}\) and \(t^2 = 9 = 2(3) + 3\) checks. Only collision point: \((3, 9)\text{,}\) at \(t = 3\text{.}\) (\((-2, 4)\) is not one: particle 1 is there at \(t = -2\text{,}\) particle 2 at \(t = \frac12\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. (4 points).

    An object moves along the curve
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t^2,\ 2t,\ \ln t \right\rangle, \qquad t \gt 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and parametric equations of the tangent line to the curve at the point \((1, 2, 0)\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle 2t,\ 2,\ \frac1t \right\rangle\text{;}\) \((1, 2, 0) = \mathbf{r}(1)\) and \(\mathbf{v}(1) = \langle 2, 2, 1 \rangle\text{,}\) so the tangent line is \(\langle 1, 2, 0 \rangle + s\,\langle 2, 2, 1 \rangle\text{,}\) i.e. \(x = 1 + 2s\text{,}\) \(y = 2 + 2s\text{,}\) \(z = s\) (\(s \in \mathbb{R}\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the distance traveled by the object from the point \((1, 2, 0)\) to the point \(\left(e^2, 2e, 1\right)\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Speed (with \(\mathbf{v}\) from (a)), for \(t \gt 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \lVert \mathbf{v}(t) \rVert \amp = \sqrt{4t^2 + 4 + \frac{1}{t^2}}\\ \amp = \sqrt{\left(2t + \frac1t\right)^2} = 2t + \frac1t . \end{align*}
    The points are \(\mathbf{r}(1)\) and \(\mathbf{r}(e)\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} L \amp = \int_1^e \left(2t + \frac1t\right) dt = \Bigl[\, t^2 + \ln t \,\Bigr]_1^e\\ \amp = \left(e^2 + 1\right) - (1 + 0) = e^2 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. (3 points).

    (a)

    A wall stands on the quarter of the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) that runs from \((2, 0)\) to \((0, 2)\) in the first quadrant. Its height above the point \((x, y)\) is \(h(x, y) = 1 + xy\text{,}\) where all lengths are in meters. Find the area of one side of the wall. (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t \rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le \frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) and \(\lVert \mathbf{v}(t) \rVert = \lVert \langle -2\sin t, 2\cos t \rangle \rVert = 2\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \text{Area} \amp = \int_C (1 + xy)\, ds\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi/2} (1 + 4\cos t\sin t)\, 2\, dt\\ \amp = \pi + 8\left[\frac{\sin^2 t}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \pi + 4 \ \text{m}^2 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    A thin wire has the shape of the whole circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{,}\) and its density at the point \((x, y)\) is \(\rho(x, y) = 3 + x\) grams per meter. Find the mass of the wire. (1 point)
    🔗
    Solution.
    With \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t, 2\sin t \rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{,}\) and \(\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = 2\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \int_C (3 + x)\, ds\\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi} (3 + 2\cos t)\, 2\, dt\\ \amp = 12\pi + 4\Bigl[\, \sin t \,\Bigr]_0^{2\pi} = 12\pi \ \text{g}. \end{align*}
    (Or: \(\int_C 3\, ds = 3 \cdot (\text{length}) = 3 \cdot 4\pi\text{,}\) and \(\int_C x\, ds = 0\) since the circle is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis and \(x\) changes sign under that reflection.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. (2 points).

    An object moves once counterclockwise around the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\) (in meters). Throughout the motion it is acted on by a force \(\mathbf{F}\) of constant magnitude \(\lVert \mathbf{F} \rVert = 4\) newtons that always makes an angle of \(60^\circ\) with the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{T}\) of the path (Figure 3.6.1). Find the work done by \(\mathbf{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    At every point
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T} = \lVert \mathbf{F} \rVert\, \lVert \mathbf{T} \rVert \cos 60^\circ = 4 \cdot 1 \cdot \frac12 = 2, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{align*} W \amp = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds = \oint_C 2\, ds\\ \amp = 2 \cdot (\text{length of } C) = 2 \cdot 2\pi(3) = 12\pi \ \text{J}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. (4 points).

    Consider the vector field
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F}(x, y, z) \amp = (2xy + z)\,\mathbf{i} + \left(x^2 + 2y\right)\mathbf{j}\\ \amp \qquad + (x + 2z)\,\mathbf{k}. \end{align*}
    Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) where \(C\) is the curve
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{r}(t) \amp = \left\langle t^2,\ 1 + t^3,\ \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) \right\rangle,\\ \amp \qquad 0 \le t \le 1 . \end{align*}
    Remember to include all the steps of your work.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Potential \(f\) (\(\nabla f = \mathbf{F}\)):
    \begin{align*} \amp f_x = 2xy + z\\ \Rightarrow\quad \amp f = x^2 y + xz + g(y, z);\\ \amp f_y = x^2 + g_y = x^2 + 2y\\ \Rightarrow\quad \amp g = y^2 + h(z);\\ \amp f_z = x + h'(z) = x + 2z\\ \Rightarrow\quad \amp h = z^2 + K. \end{align*}
    So \(f = x^2 y + xz + y^2 + z^2\) (plus any constant); check:
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f = \left\langle 2xy + z,\ x^2 + 2y,\ x + 2z \right\rangle = \mathbf{F}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\nabla f\) is continuous everywhere and \(C\) is smooth, the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals applies, with \(A = \mathbf{r}(0) = (0, 1, 0)\) and \(B = \mathbf{r}(1) = (1, 2, 1)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = f(B) - f(A)\\ \amp = \bigl(1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 + 4 + 1\bigr)\\ \amp \qquad - \bigl(0 + 0 + 1 + 0\bigr) = 7 . \end{align*}
    (A direct computation from the parametrization gives the same value but is much longer.)

    6. (4 points).

    Decide whether each statement below is true or false. Clearly justify your answer: a correct answer with an incorrect or unclear justification will not earn credit.
    🔗

    (a)

    True or False? The vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x, y) = y^2\,\mathbf{i} + xy\,\mathbf{j}\) is conservative. (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. \(\mathbf{F}\) is continuous on the open connected region \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{,}\) so it is conservative iff it is a gradient field. If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\text{:}\) \(f_x = y^2 \Rightarrow f = xy^2 + g(y)\text{,}\) and \(f_y = 2xy + g'(y) = xy\) forces \(g'(y) = -xy\text{,}\) impossible since \(g'(y)\) depends on \(y\) only. No potential exists: not conservative. (Other valid arguments: \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\) would give \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(y^2\right) = f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy)\) by the Mixed Derivative Theorem, but \(2y \ne y\text{;}\) or two paths with the same endpoints and different line integrals, e.g. \(\frac23\) along the segment from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 1)\) and \(\frac35\) along \(y = x^2\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    True or False? If a vector field \(\mathbf{G}\) satisfies \(\oint_C \mathbf{G} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) for some closed curve \(C\text{,}\) then \(\mathbf{G}\) is conservative. (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    False. Counterexample: \(\mathbf{F}\) of part (a) and the unit circle \(C\) given by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t \rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{.}\) Then \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \left\langle \sin^2 t,\ \cos t\sin t \right\rangle\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -\sin t, \cos t \rangle\text{,}\) and, using \(\sin^2 t = 1 - \cos^2 t\) and \(u = \cos t\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \left(-\sin^3 t + \cos^2 t\sin t\right) dt\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \left(2\cos^2 t - 1\right)\sin t\, dt\\ \amp \quad = \Bigl[\, -\tfrac23\cos^3 t + \cos t \,\Bigr]_0^{2\pi} = 0, \end{align*}
    yet \(\mathbf{F}\) is not conservative by (a). The loop property requires \(\oint_C \mathbf{G} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) around every closed curve, not just one.
