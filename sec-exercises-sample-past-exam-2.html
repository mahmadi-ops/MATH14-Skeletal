    1.

    Rewrite (do not evaluate) the following integral in cylindrical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^4\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{4-(y-2)^2}}^{\sqrt{4-(y-2)^2}}\!\! \int_0^{\sqrt{16-x^2-y^2}} xyz\,dz\,dx\,dy . \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The limits on \(x\) and \(y\) describe the projection of the solid onto the \(xy\)-plane: \(0 \le y \le 4\) and \(x^2 \le 4 - (y-2)^2\text{,}\) that is, the disk \(x^2 + (y-2)^2 \le 4\) of radius \(2\) centered at \((0,2)\) (Figure 3.11.1). Its boundary circle is \(x^2 + y^2 = 4y\text{,}\) in polar coordinates \(r^2 = 4r\sin\theta\text{,}\) so \(r = 4\sin\theta\text{;}\) the disk is swept out by \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\text{,}\) \(0 \le r \le 4\sin\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The limits on \(z\) run from the plane \(z = 0\) up to \(z = \sqrt{16 - x^2 - y^2} = \sqrt{16 - r^2}\text{,}\) the upper half of the sphere of radius \(4\text{.}\) The integrand is \(xyz = (r\cos\theta)(r\sin\theta)\,z = r^2 z\cos\theta\sin\theta\text{,}\) and \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\) by (2.6.2). Hence
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \int_0^4\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{4-(y-2)^2}}^{\sqrt{4-(y-2)^2}}\!\! \int_0^{\sqrt{16-x^2-y^2}} xyz\,dz\,dx\,dy\\ \quad = \int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{4\sin\theta}\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt{16-r^2}} r^3 z\cos\theta\sin\theta\,dz\,dr\,d\theta . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x^2}\!\!\int_0^{y} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx\text{.}\) Rewrite (do not evaluate) this integral in the following forms.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dx\,dz\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The given limits describe the solid
    \begin{equation*} R:\quad 0 \le x \le 1,\qquad 0 \le y \le x^2,\qquad 0 \le z \le y , \end{equation*}
    shown in Figure 3.11.2: it stands on the region of the \(xy\)-plane between the parabola \(y = x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis, up to the plane \(z = y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For the order \(dy\,dx\,dz\) the outer variable is \(z\text{,}\) which runs over \(0 \le z \le 1\) in all. For a fixed \(z\) the solid is the slice at that height: the condition \(z \le y \le x^2\) needs \(x^2 \ge z\text{,}\) that is, \(\sqrt{z} \le x \le 1\text{,}\) and then \(y\) runs from \(z\) up to \(x^2\text{.}\) The projection of \(R\) onto the \(xz\)-plane is the region under the parabola \(z = x^2\) (Figure 3.11.3), and
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dx\,dz = \int_0^1\!\!\int_{\sqrt z}^{1}\!\!\int_{z}^{x^2} f(x,y,z)\,dy\,dx\,dz . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dz\,dy\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Now the outer variable is \(y\text{,}\) with \(0 \le y \le 1\) in all; for a fixed \(y\text{,}\) \(z\) runs from \(0\) to \(y\text{,}\) and the inner variable \(x\) must satisfy \(y \le x^2\text{,}\) that is, \(\sqrt{y} \le x \le 1\text{.}\) The projection of \(R\) onto the \(yz\)-plane is the triangle \(0 \le z \le y \le 1\) (Figure 3.11.4), and
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_R f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dz\,dy = \int_0^1\!\!\int_{0}^{y}\!\!\int_{\sqrt y}^{1} f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dz\,dy . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Set up (do not evaluate) a double integral for the purpose of computing the area outside \(\left(x^2+y^2\right)^{3/2} = 2x^2\) and inside \((x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both curves are simplest in polar coordinates. Since \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\text{,}\) the first is \(r^3 = 2r^2\cos^2\theta\text{,}\) that is, \(r = 2\cos^2\theta\text{.}\) The second is \(x^2 + y^2 = 2x\text{,}\) that is, \(r^2 = 2r\cos\theta\text{,}\) the circle \(r = 2\cos\theta\) of radius \(1\) centered at \((1,0)\text{.}\) Replacing \(\theta\) by \(-\theta\) changes neither equation, so both curves are symmetric about the \(x\)-axis, and both are traced once as \(\theta\) runs from \(-\pi/2\) to \(\pi/2\text{:}\) a few values of \(r = 2\cos^2\theta\) are \(2\) at \(\theta = 0\text{,}\) \(3/2\) at \(\pi/6\text{,}\) \(1\) at \(\pi/4\text{,}\) \(1/2\) at \(\pi/3\text{,}\) and \(0\) at \(\pi/2\) (Figure 3.11.5).
    🔗
    For \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\) we have \(0 \le \cos\theta \le 1\text{,}\) so \(2\cos^2\theta \le 2\cos\theta\text{:}\) along a ray at angle \(\theta\) the region runs from the curve out to the circle. With \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) by (2.4.1), and using the symmetry about the \(x\)-axis,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_{2\cos^2\theta}^{2\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_{2\cos^2\theta}^{2\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Rewrite (do not evaluate) the following integral in spherical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-2}^{0}\!\!\int_{0}^{\sqrt{4-y^2}}\!\! \int_{-\sqrt{8-x^2-y^2}}^{-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} xyz\,dz\,dx\,dy . \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The projection. The limits \(-2 \le y \le 0\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le \sqrt{4 - y^2}\) describe the quarter of the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) with \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \le 0\text{,}\) the fourth quadrant (Figure 3.11.6): the rays through it have \(3\pi/2 \le \theta \le 2\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The solid. The lower limit on \(z\text{,}\) \(z = -\sqrt{8 - x^2 - y^2}\text{,}\) is the lower half of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 8\text{,}\) which is \(\rho = 2\sqrt2\text{;}\) the upper limit, \(z = -\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\text{,}\) is the lower nappe of the cone \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) which is \(\phi = 3\pi/4\) (see (2.7.1): on it \(\rho\cos\phi = -\rho\sin\phi\text{,}\) so \(\tan\phi = -1\) with \(\phi\) in the lower half). The solid lies below the cone and above the sphere, so it is the part of the ball \(\rho \le 2\sqrt2\) with \(3\pi/4 \le \phi \le \pi\text{,}\) an ice-cream cone pointing down, cut to the quarter \(3\pi/2 \le \theta \le 2\pi\) (Figure 3.11.7). As a check, the cone and the sphere meet where \(x^2 + y^2 = 8 - (x^2 + y^2)\text{,}\) that is, on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{,}\) which is exactly the boundary of the projection.
    🔗
    The integral. With \(x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\text{,}\) \(y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\text{,}\) \(z = \rho\cos\phi\text{,}\) the integrand is
    \begin{equation*} xyz = \rho^3\sin^2\phi\cos\phi\,\sin\theta\cos\theta , \end{equation*}
    and \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\) by (2.7.2). Hence
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \int_{-2}^{0}\!\!\int_{0}^{\sqrt{4-y^2}}\!\! \int_{-\sqrt{8-x^2-y^2}}^{-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} xyz\,dz\,dx\,dy\\ \quad = \int_{3\pi/2}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{3\pi/4}^{\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\sqrt2} \rho^5\sin^3\phi\cos\phi\,\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta . \end{gather*}
    🔗

    5.

    Without calculating the integrals, decide whether each of the following integrals is positive, negative, or zero. Let \(S\) be the solid bounded by \(z = (x-2)^2\text{,}\) \(z = 2 - (x-2)^2\text{,}\) \(y = -1\text{,}\) and \(y = 1\text{.}\) Remember to justify your responses.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S \sin^5(x-3)\,dV\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Negative. First locate \(S\text{.}\) The two parabolic cylinders meet where \((x-2)^2 = 2 - (x-2)^2\text{,}\) that is, \((x-2)^2 = 1\text{,}\) so at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\text{.}\) Between them the second lies above the first, so
    \begin{equation*} S:\quad 1 \le x \le 3,\qquad -1 \le y \le 1,\qquad (x-2)^2 \le z \le 2-(x-2)^2 , \end{equation*}
    a solid with the cross-section of Figure 3.11.8 in every plane \(y = c\text{,}\) \(-1 \le c \le 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On \(S\) we have \(1 \le x \le 3\text{,}\) so \(-2 \le x - 3 \le 0\text{.}\) On the interval \([-2, 0]\text{,}\) which lies inside \([-\pi, 0]\text{,}\) the sine is at most \(0\text{,}\) so \(\sin(x-3) \le 0\) and therefore \(\sin^5(x-3) \le 0\) throughout \(S\text{,}\) with equality only on the face \(x = 3\text{.}\) An integrand that is negative on the solid except on a face of zero volume has a negative integral.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S \sin^3(x-2)\,dV\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Zero. The solid, described in the solution of part (a), is symmetric about the plane \(x = 2\) (Figure 3.11.8): replacing \(x - 2\) by \(-(x-2)\) changes none of its bounding surfaces, so the reflection \(x \mapsto 4 - x\) maps \(S\) onto itself. The integrand is odd under the same reflection,
    \begin{equation*} \sin^3\bigl((4-x) - 2\bigr) = \sin^3\bigl(-(x-2)\bigr) = -\sin^3(x-2): \end{equation*}
    it is \(\ge 0\) on the half \(x \ge 2\) of \(S\) (there \(0 \le x - 2 \le 1\)) and \(\le 0\) on the half \(x \le 2\text{,}\) with the values on the two halves mirror images of each other. The two halves contribute opposite amounts and the integral is \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    True or False? If the statement is true, prove it; if it is false, give a counterexample.
    🔗
    Let \(E\) be the solid unit ball \(E = \{(x,y,z) : x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1\}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\,dV = \frac13\operatorname{Vol}(E) . \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    True. Write
    \begin{equation*} I_x = \iiint_E \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\,dV , \end{equation*}
    and define \(I_y\) and \(I_z\) in the same way, with \(y^2\) and \(z^2\) in the numerator.
    🔗
    Use symmetry. The unit ball is unchanged when the coordinate axes are permuted. Swapping \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) that is, the change of variables \((x,y,z) \mapsto (y,x,z)\text{,}\) maps \(E\) onto itself, does not change volumes (its Jacobian determinant is \(-1\)), and turns the integrand of \(I_x\) into that of \(I_y\text{,}\) since the denominator \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) is unchanged. So \(I_x = I_y\text{,}\) and swapping \(x\) and \(z\) in the same way gives \(I_x = I_z\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} I_x = I_y = I_z . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Add the three equal integrals.
    \begin{align*} I_x + I_y + I_z \amp = \iiint_E \frac{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\,dV\\ \amp = \iiint_E 1\,dV = \operatorname{Vol}(E) . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Conclude. Since the three integrals are equal,
    \begin{equation*} 3I_x = \operatorname{Vol}(E) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_x = \frac13\operatorname{Vol}(E) . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Numerical value. Since \(\operatorname{Vol}(E) = \tfrac43\pi\text{,}\) the integral is \(\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac43\pi = \tfrac{4\pi}{9}\text{.}\)
