    1. A Curl Integral Over a Hemisphere.

    Solution.

    Curling this field and integrating over the cap is a great deal of work for nothing. Stokes’ theorem replaces the cap by its boundary curve \(C\text{,}\) the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = R^2\) in the plane \(z = 0\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise seen from above to match the upward \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{.}\) On \(C\) we have \(z = 0\text{,}\) so the third component of \(\mathbf{F}\) never contributes:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\\ \amp \quad = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \oint_C a\,y\, dx - a\,x\, dy . \end{align*}
    With \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle R\cos t,\ R\sin t,\ 0 \right\rangle\) the integrand is \(-aR^2\sin^2 t - aR^2\cos^2 t = -aR^2\text{,}\) a constant, so the integral is \(-2\pi aR^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Green’s theorem, with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\text{.}\) The cap is not the only surface with boundary \(C\text{:}\) so is the flat disk \(D\text{,}\) \(x^2 + y^2 \le R^2\) in the plane \(z = 0\text{.}\) Take its normal to be \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) pointing up, which gives \(C\) the same counterclockwise direction as the cap does. By (3.2.2) both fluxes of the curl equal \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) so they are equal (see Subsection 3.2.3), and we may integrate over \(D\) instead of the cap. On \(D\text{,}\) \(d\sigma = dA\text{,}\) and dotting with \(\mathbf{k}\) keeps only the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl. Writing \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j} + P\,\mathbf{k}\) with \(M = a\,y\) and \(N = -a\,x\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k} \amp = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\\ \amp = -a - a = -2a . \end{align*}
    This is exactly the integrand of the circulation form of Green’s theorem, Theorem 3.1.17: Green’s theorem is Stokes’ theorem for a flat surface with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\text{.}\) The term \(yx^3\) drops out, since it only feeds the \(\mathbf{i}\)- and \(\mathbf{j}\)-components of the curl. Since \(D\) has area \(\pi R^2\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\\ \amp \quad = \iint_D (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\, dA\\ \amp \quad = \iint_D (-2a)\, dA = -2a\pi R^2 , \end{align*}
    the same answer as the line integral, with no parametrization at all.
    🔗

    2. Verifying Stokes’ Theorem on a Hemisphere.

    Solution.

    First, \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left\langle -1,\ -1,\ -1 \right\rangle\text{,}\) a constant field.
    🔗
    For the surface integral, close the hemisphere with the flat disk \(x^2 + z^2 \le R^2\) in the plane \(y = 0\text{.}\) A constant field has zero divergence, so the total flux out of that closed surface is \(0\text{:}\) the flux through the hemisphere is minus the flux through the disk. The disk’s outward normal is \(-\mathbf{j}\) and its area is \(\pi R^2\text{,}\) so its flux is \(\left\langle -1, -1, -1 \right\rangle \cdot \left(-\mathbf{j}\right)\pi R^2 = \pi R^2\text{,}\) and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = -\pi R^2 . \end{equation*}
    The boundary curve \(C\) is the circle of radius \(R\) in the plane \(y = 0\text{.}\) Parametrize it by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle R\cos t,\ 0,\ -R\sin t \right\rangle\text{,}\) the direction that matches the outward \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{.}\) Then \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle 0,\ -R\sin t,\ R\cos t \right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle -R\sin t,\ 0,\ -R\cos t \right\rangle\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} -R^2\cos^2 t\, dt = -\pi R^2 , \end{equation*}
    which is the same number. Stokes’ theorem checks out.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Verifying Stokes’ Theorem on a Cone.

    Solution.

    Here \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left\langle 0,\ 0,\ 2a \right\rangle\text{;}\) the constant third component of \(\mathbf{F}\) curls to nothing.
    🔗
    Parametrize the cone by \(\mathbf{r}(u, \theta) = \left\langle u\cos\theta,\ u\sin\theta,\ u \right\rangle\) with \(0 \le u \le h\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_\theta = \left\langle -u\cos\theta,\ -u\sin\theta,\ u \right\rangle , \end{equation*}
    whose third component is positive, so this is already the upward orientation. Only that third component meets the curl, giving
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{h} 2au\, du\, d\theta = 2a\pi h^2 . \end{equation*}
    The boundary curve \(C\) is the circle of radius \(h\) in the plane \(z = h\text{,}\) counterclockwise seen from above: \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle h\cos t,\ h\sin t,\ h \right\rangle\text{.}\) Then \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = ah^2\sin^2 t + ah^2\cos^2 t = ah^2\text{,}\) so the circulation is \(2a\pi h^2\) as well.
    🔗
    Notice that the answer never sees \(b\text{,}\) and that it is the same as the curl integral over the flat disk capping the cone: Stokes’ theorem only cares about the boundary.
