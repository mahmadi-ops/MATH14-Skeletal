    1. Mass of a Cone Along the \(x\)-Axis.

    An object is bounded by \(x = \sqrt{y^2+z^2}\) and \(x = 1\text{,}\) and its density is \(\rho(x,y,z) = x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The mass of the object is .
    🔗

    Solution.

    With \(dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dx\) (the cylindrical volume element (2.6.2) about the \(x\)-axis) and \(r \le x \le 1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_r^1 x\,r\,dx\,d\theta\,dr = (2\pi)\left(\tfrac12\right)\int_0^1 r\left(1-r^2\right) dr\\ \amp = \pi\left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2. From Cylindrical Back to Rectangular.

    Convert \(\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/4}^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^{\sec\theta} \!\!\int_0^{r^2\cos(2\theta)} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\) to rectangular coordinates.
    🔗
    In rectangular coordinates the innermost limit for \(z\) becomes ; for a fixed \(x\text{,}\) \(y\) runs from to ; and \(x\) runs from \(0\) to .
    🔗

    Solution.

    Since \(r^2\cos(2\theta) = r^2\left(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta\right) = x^2 - y^2\) and \(r = \sec\theta\) is \(r\cos\theta = 1\text{,}\) that is \(x = 1\text{,}\) the region in the plane is the triangle bounded by \(y = x\text{,}\) \(y = -x\text{,}\) and \(x = 1\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1\!\!\int_{-x}^{x}\!\!\int_0^{x^2-y^2} dz\,dy\,dx . \end{equation*}

    3. Two Signs, No Calculation.

    Let \(R\) be the solid between \(z = x^2+y^2\) and \(z = 1-x^2-y^2\text{.}\) Type positive, negative, or zero for each.
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R \left(z+x^2+y^2-1\right) dV\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \(\displaystyle\iiint_R \left(z-x^2-y^2\right) dV\text{:}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    On \(R\text{,}\) \(x^2+y^2 \le z \le 1-x^2-y^2\text{.}\) The right inequality gives \(z + x^2 + y^2 - 1 \le 0\text{,}\) so the first integral is negative; the left gives \(z - x^2 - y^2 \ge 0\text{,}\) so the second is positive. Each integrand vanishes only on one of the two bounding surfaces.

    4. Volume Between a Hyperboloid and a Paraboloid.

    Find the volume of the region bounded by \(x^2+y^2-z^2+5 = 0\) and \(x^2+y^2+z-1 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The two surfaces meet at \(z = \) , over the circle of radius , and the volume is .
    🔗

    Solution.

    The surfaces are \(z = \pm\sqrt{r^2+5}\) and \(z = 1-r^2\text{.}\) Setting \(r^2 = z^2-5\) equal to \(r^2 = 1-z\) gives \(z^2+z-6 = 0\text{,}\) so \(z = 2\) or \(z = -3\text{;}\) only \(z = -3\) is possible, and then \(r^2 = 4\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{r^2+5}}^{1-r^2} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta \approx 20.566 . \end{equation*}

    The solid of integration is half of a ball; its radius is , and the value of the integral is .
    🔗

    Solution.

    The bounds describe the hemisphere \(\rho \le 6\text{,}\) \(x \ge 0\text{.}\) Since \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\, d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\) by (2.7.2) and the integrand is \(1/\rho\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^6 \rho \sin\phi \,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta \amp = \pi \cdot \frac{6^2}{2}\cdot 2\\ \amp = 36\pi \approx 113.097 . \end{align*}

    6. Surfaces in Spherical Coordinates.

    Describe each surface, given in rectangular coordinates, in spherical coordinates.
    🔗
    (a) The plane \(x = \sqrt3\,y\) is the union of the two half-planes \(\theta = \) and \(\theta = \) (the smaller angle first; both between \(0\) and \(2\pi\)).
    🔗
    (b) The cone \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\) is the union of the two cones \(\phi = \) and \(\phi = \) (the smaller angle first).
    🔗
    (c) On the ellipsoid \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2/4 = 1\text{,}\) write the equation in spherical coordinates and solve it for \(\rho\) in terms of \(\phi\text{.}\) On the \(z\)-axis (\(\phi = 0\)) the surface is at \(\rho = \), in the \(xy\)-plane (\(\phi = \pi/2\)) at \(\rho = \), and at \(\phi = \pi/4\) at \(\rho = \).
    🔗

    Solution.

    Substitute \(x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\text{,}\) \(y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\text{,}\) \(z = \rho\cos\phi\) ((2.7.1)) in each equation.
    🔗
    (a) \(x = \sqrt3\,y\) becomes \(\rho\sin\phi\,(\cos\theta - \sqrt3\sin\theta) = 0\text{.}\) Off the \(z\)-axis this says \(\tan\theta = 1/\sqrt3\text{,}\) so \(\theta = \pi/6\) or \(\theta = 7\pi/6\text{;}\) the \(z\)-axis itself (\(\rho\sin\phi = 0\)) lies in the plane and on both half-planes. The plane is vertical, so it is determined by its trace in the \(xy\)-plane, the line through the origin at angle \(\pi/6\) (Figure 3.9.1).
    🔗
    (b) \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\) becomes \(\rho^2\cos^2\phi = \rho^2\sin^2\phi\text{,}\) that is \(\tan^2\phi = 1\) with \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\text{:}\) the upper nappe \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is \(\phi = \pi/4\) and the lower nappe \(z = -\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is \(\phi = 3\pi/4\) (Figure 3.9.2). (Geometrically: every point of the cone has \(|z| = r\text{,}\) so the line from the origin to it makes a \(45^\circ\) angle with the \(z\)-axis.)
    🔗
    (c) Since \(x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2\sin^2\phi\) and \(z^2 = \rho^2\cos^2\phi\text{,}\) the ellipsoid becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \rho^2\sin^2\phi + \frac{\rho^2\cos^2\phi}{4} = 1, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \rho^2\,(4\sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi) = 4 . \end{equation*}
    With \(\cos^2\phi = 1 - \sin^2\phi\) this is \(\rho^2 = \dfrac{4}{1 + 3\sin^2\phi}\text{,}\) that is, \(\rho = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2\phi}}\text{:}\) on the ellipsoid the distance from the origin depends on \(\phi\) alone. On the \(z\)-axis, \(\phi = 0\) gives \(\rho = 2\text{;}\) in the \(xy\)-plane, \(\phi = \pi/2\) gives \(\rho = 1\text{;}\) and at \(\phi = \pi/4\text{,}\) \(\sin^2\phi = 1/2\) gives \(\rho = 2/\sqrt{5/2} = \sqrt{8/5} \approx 1.265\text{.}\) The distance shrinks from \(2\) at the poles to \(1\) at the equator (Figure 3.9.3).

    7. A Cap Cut From a Ball.

    Find the volume of the region bounded by the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\) and the plane \(z = 1\) (the part of the ball that lies above the plane).
    🔗
    In spherical coordinates the region is \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\text{,}\) \(0 \le \phi \le \), and \(\rho\) runs from a lower limit that depends on \(\phi\) (at \(\phi = \pi/4\) that limit equals ) up to . The volume is \(V = \).
    🔗

    Solution.

    The sphere is \(\rho = 2\text{,}\) and the plane \(z = 1\) is \(\rho\cos\phi = 1\text{,}\) that is \(\rho = \sec\phi\text{.}\) They meet where \(2\cos\phi = 1\text{,}\) so \(\phi = \pi/3\text{;}\) that is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 3\text{,}\) \(z = 1\text{.}\) A ray from the origin at angle \(\phi \le \pi/3\) enters the region through the plane at \(\rho = \sec\phi\) and leaves it through the sphere at \(\rho = 2\) (Figure 3.9.4 and Figure 3.9.5), so the region is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi,\qquad 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{3},\qquad \sec\phi \le \rho \le 2 . \end{equation*}
    At \(\phi = \pi/4\) the lower limit is \(\sec(\pi/4) = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414\text{.}\)
    🔗
    With \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\) by (2.7.2),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/3}\!\!\int_{\sec\phi}^{2} \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^{\pi/3} \frac{8 - \sec^3\phi}{3}\,\sin\phi\,d\phi\\ \amp = \frac{2\pi}{3}\left[-8\cos\phi - \frac{1}{2\cos^2\phi}\right]_0^{\pi/3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}\left[(-4 - 2) - \left(-8 - \tfrac12\right)\right]\\ \amp = \frac{2\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{5}{2} = \frac{5\pi}{3} \approx 5.236 , \end{align*}
    where \(\int \sec^3\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = \int \cos^{-3}\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = \dfrac{1}{2\cos^2\phi}\text{.}\) (Check: a cap of height \(h\) on a ball of radius \(R\) has volume \(\pi h^2(3R - h)/3\text{;}\) with \(R = 2\text{,}\) \(h = 1\) this is \(5\pi/3\text{.}\))

    8. An Integral Over an Ice-Cream Cone.

    Let \(R\) be the region above the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) and below the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\text{.}\) Compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_R z^2\,dV . \end{equation*}
    In spherical coordinates \(R\) is \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\text{,}\) \(0 \le \phi \le \), \(0 \le \rho \le \), and the integral equals .
    🔗

    Solution.

    The cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is \(\phi = \pi/4\) and the sphere is \(\rho = 1\text{,}\) so \(R\) is the ice-cream cone
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi,\qquad 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{4},\qquad 0 \le \rho \le 1 \end{equation*}
    of Figure 3.9.6 and Figure 3.9.7.
    🔗
    With \(z = \rho\cos\phi\) and \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iiint_R z^2\,dV \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^{1} \rho^2\cos^2\phi\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp = 2\pi\left(\int_0^1 \rho^4\,d\rho\right) \left(\int_0^{\pi/4}\cos^2\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi\right) = 2\pi\cdot\frac15\cdot\left[-\frac{\cos^3\phi}{3}\right]_0^{\pi/4}\\ \amp = \frac{2\pi}{15}\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi\,(4 - \sqrt2)}{30} \approx 0.271 . \end{align*}

    9. A Wedge Cut From a Cylinder.

    Solution.

    Write \(R\) for the radius of the cylinder; the wedge is shown in Figure 3.9.8.
    🔗
    The wedge is bounded below by \(z = 0\) and above by \(z = x\text{,}\) so \(0 \le z \le x\text{;}\) in particular \(x \ge 0\text{,}\) and the wedge projects onto the half disk \(\mathcal{D}\) of radius \(R\) with \(x \ge 0\) (Figure 3.9.9). In polar coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{D}:\ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2},\ 0 \le r \le R , \end{equation*}
    and the top surface is \(z = x = r\cos\theta\text{.}\) So in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathcal{W}:\ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2},\ 0 \le r \le R,\ 0 \le z \le r\cos\theta . \end{equation*}
    The volume is \(\iiint_{\mathcal{W}} 1\,dV\text{,}\) and with \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\) by (2.6.2),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V \amp = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{R}\!\!\int_0^{r\cos\theta} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{R} r^2\cos\theta\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp = \frac{R^3}{3}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos\theta\,d\theta = \frac{R^3}{3}\Big[\sin\theta\Big]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{2R^3}{3} . \end{align*}
    For instance, a cylinder of radius \(3\) gives \(V = 2\cdot 27/3 = 18\text{.}\)
